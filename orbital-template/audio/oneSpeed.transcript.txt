A candle flame in your kitchen produces light. A distant quasar burning at the edge of the observable universe produces light. A gamma-ray burst from a collapsing star, unleashing more energy in seconds than the sun will release in its entire lifetime, produces light.
Every one of these photons, the calm ones, the violent ones, the ones so faint they can barely be measured, and the ones so energetic they could shatter atomic nuclei, travels at exactly the same speed through empty space. Not approximately the same speed. Exactly.
The photon that just left the flickering candle beside you is racing forward at the same velocity as the photon that has been streaking across the cosmos for 13 billion years. Nothing pushes them, nothing accelerates them. They are never slow, never fast, only this one speed.

Consider just how strange the situation actually is. Every source of light in the universe answers to entirely different rules.
A candle flame produces light through a chemical reaction at a temperature of perhaps 1,500 degrees. A hydrogen atom sitting in a cold interstellar cloud produces a photon by dropping an electron between two quantum energy levels, releasing a tiny sliver of radio energy. A star like our sun produces light from nuclear fusion at core temperatures near 15 million degrees.
A gamma-ray burst, one of the most violent events astronomers have ever detected, releases photons carrying so much energy that a single one of them could rip a hole through the strongest metals we know how to make.
These are not variations on a theme. These are radically different physical processes.
The reactants are different. The temperatures are different. The energies per photon differ by factors of trillions. The physical mechanisms could not be more distinct from one another.
And yet, the moment each of those processes produces a photon, the photon that emerges is moving at exactly the same speed through empty space.
Not approximately the same speed. Not statistically similar. Exactly the same speed, to as many decimal places as any experiment has ever probed.
The photon from your candle is moving at 299,792,458 meters per second.
So is the photon from the gamma-ray burst. So is the photon from the hydrogen atom in the interstellar cloud. So is the light from every screen you have ever looked at.
In vacuum, they all move at the same speed. There are no fast photons. There are no slow photons. There are only photons moving.
To appreciate how strange this actually is, think about how nearly everything else in nature behaves.
Throw a baseball and its speed depends on how hard you throw it. Fire a bullet from different rifles and the muzzle velocity varies from firearm to firearm. Push a car from rest and the speed climbs gradually as the engine does work. Kick a soccer ball softly and it drifts. Kick it hard and it rockets.
